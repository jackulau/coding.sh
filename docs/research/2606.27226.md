- **Paper:** Sangwoo Cho, Kushal Chawla, Pengshan Cai, Zefang Liu, Chenyang Zhu, Shi-Xiong Zhang,
  Sambit Sahu (Capital One, AI Foundations). *Ask, Don't Judge: Binary Questions for Interpretable LLM
  Evaluation and Self-Improvement.* arXiv:2606.27226v1, submitted 25 June 2026. Comments field:
  "Acceepted to the Second Workshop on Compositional Learning at ICML 2026" (the typo is in the
  original).
- **Links:** [abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27226) · [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.27226v1) ·
  [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.27226) · [TeX source](https://arxiv.org/src/2606.27226)
- **What was read:** the whole paper, from the TeX source (`icml2026_main.tex`, 1,420 lines) and the
  v1 HTML. That covers §1–7 and appendices A–E. Section numbers below follow the HTML rendering.
- **Code and data:** none linked. The abs page, the paper and the TeX source give no repository.
  The meta-prompt (§3.1) and the evaluator prompts are not published. Only the generated SummEval
  question sets are (App. E, Tables 9–12). The results cannot be reproduced exactly from what is
  public.

## TL;DR

1. BinEval replaces one holistic LLM rating with N atomic yes/no questions per quality dimension.
   The questions are generated once from the task prompt by a meta-prompt and answered one at a
   time, each with an explanation. The score is the fraction of "yes".
2. With Claude Sonnet 4 as the evaluator, it reports the best average rank correlation with humans
   on SummEval (ρ 0.563), Topical-Chat (ρ 0.632) and QAGS (ρ 0.620).
3. The like-for-like test runs every method on the same backbone (gpt-oss-120b). On it,
   decomposition helps a lot on QAGS (ρ 0.563 vs 0.132 for G-Eval) and roughly ties on SummEval
   (0.447 vs 0.436) and Topical-Chat (0.539 vs 0.541). The headline wins over G-Eval also mix a
   method change with a model change (Claude Sonnet 4 vs GPT-4 numbers copied from 2023).
4. Question-level disagreements drive a "lesson" loop that rewrites prompts. It gains +0.075 ρ on
   SummEval, but the best iteration is chosen on the **test** set. On IFBench the gain is +3.4 pp,
   inside the noise, and the prompt grows from 22 to 6,248 characters before it collapses.
5. An independent replication on the same benchmarks ("Ask, Then Grade", NeurIPS 2026 workshop)
   finds plain five-point ratings beat BinEval-style decomposition in 31 of 36 contrasts (§6.7).
   Binary items are worth it for atomic, checkable criteria and for diagnosis, not as a universal
   default.
6. For coding.sh, take the shape: frozen binary checklists, per-item explanations, per-item
   diagnostics, and question-level disagreement for migrating a judge. Do not take the statistics,
   the test-set model selection, or the linear-average-only aggregation. Treat the judge format
   (checklist vs graded) as a versioned choice per criterion, settled by held-out κ.

## 1. Problem

§1 frames LLM evaluation as the bottleneck of iterative development. Human evaluation is slow,
lexical metrics miss semantics, and "holistic LLM judges … often return opaque scores that are
difficult to diagnose". The specific complaint is diagnosability: "if a summary receives a mediocre
rating, it is still unclear whether the problem is factual inconsistency, weak relevance, missing
content, or poor fluency" (§1). The stated premise: "instead of asking a model for one broad
judgment, ask it a set of small, checkable questions" (§1).

## 2. Method (§3)

**Question generation (§3.1).** A task-agnostic meta-prompt M maps a task prompt T to questions
Q = F_LLM(T; M) = {q1…qN} in two steps:

- *Step 1, summarize:* T becomes a list of requirements R = {r1…rK}.
- *Step 2, decompose:* each requirement becomes one or more binary questions, where "yes"
  means satisfied. Compound requirements are split, and "each question is paired with a concise
  violation example to clarify the negative case".

The questions are partitioned by dimension (Q = ∪ Q_d). Only T changes across tasks.

**Scoring (§3.2).** An evaluator E answers f_E(x, y, q_i) ∈ {0,1} and gives an explanation e_i.
Scores are unweighted means:

- per dimension: S_d = (1/|Q_d|) Σ f_E;
- overall: S = (1/N) Σ f_E.

Both are affinely rescaled to a target range: S' = S·(b−a) + a. Every question and every dimension
has equal weight, and no question acts as a gate.

**Cross-model prompt update (§3.3, Algorithm 1 in App. C).** A source evaluator E_src (the
reference, usually stronger) and a target E_tgt answer the same questions. One iteration:

1. Find the disagreement set Δ_j = {q_i : A_src ≠ A_tgt} for each item.
2. A note-taker LLM turns the disagreements into generalized "lessons".
3. An LLM dedups the lessons, merging near-duplicates.
4. For each lesson, an updater LLM finds a substring of the target prompt and rewrites it.

The loop stops when |S_d^tgt − S_d^src| < ε for all d. The paper's motivating use is "migrating a
model to a different family of models" (§3.3).

**Self prompt update (§3.4).** The same loop improves a *generator's* prompt. The evaluator's
failed questions and their explanations are the input to lesson extraction. It stops when no
evaluation errors remain or the iteration cap is reached.

## 3. Setup (§4, App. B)

- **Models:** gpt-oss-120b and Claude Sonnet 4, at "temperature 0 in all experiments", reporting
  "the average over two runs" (§4).
- **Metric:** Spearman ρ, Kendall τ and Pearson r against human ratings, "at the summary level"
  (§4.1). The prompt-update experiments use "pooled Spearman rank correlation across all test
  items rather than a per-document average" (App. B). These are two different estimators, so
  Table 2's baselines cannot be compared directly with Table 1.
- **Part I benchmarks** (UniEval protocol, §4.2):
  - SummEval: 100 CNN/DM articles × 16 systems = 1,600 summaries, 4 dimensions, 1–5 Likert.
  - Topical-Chat: described as "60 dialogue responses generated by 6 dialogue models", 4 of 6
    aspects used.
  - QAGS: 235 CNN/DM + 239 XSum summaries, consistency only.
- **Part II** (§4.3, App. B):
  - SummEval evaluator-prompt update: 160 dev / 1,440 test, seed 42, up to 5 iterations, at most
    10 unique lessons per iteration.
  - IFBench generator-prompt update: "290 test cases spanning 56 constraint types across 7
    categories", with a dev set of 56 (one per type, "preferring previously failed cases") and a
    test set of "the remaining 238".

## 4. Results with numbers

### 4.1 Evaluation quality (Part I)

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| UniEval (T5) | .575/.442 | .446/.371 | .449/.371 | .426/.325 | .474/.377 |
| G-Eval (GPT-4) | .582/.457 | .507/.425 | .506/.455 ¹ | **.547/.433** | .514/.418 |
| G-Eval (gpt-oss) | .451/.392 | .559/.527 | .217/.203 | .515/.446 | .436/.392 |
| UniEval (gpt-oss) | .237/.208 | .489/.476 | .000/.000 | .288/.256 | .254/.235 |
| BinEval (gpt-oss) | .523/.448 | .585/.548 | .252/.235 | .428/.366 | .447/.399 |
| BinEval (Claude) | **.652/.541** | **.655/.615** | **.540/.470** | .404/.339 | **.563/.491** |

¹ The G-Eval paper (arXiv 2303.16634, Table 1) reports G-Eval-4 fluency as 0.455 / 0.378, not
0.506 / 0.455. The BinEval row's own average (.514) also does not equal the mean of its cells
(.536). See §6.5.

- **Topical-Chat** (App. D.1, Table 7): BinEval (Claude) has the best average ρ, 0.632. It is best
  on naturalness (.686) and engagingness (.740). UniEval (T5) and G-Eval stay stronger on coherence
  (.602 / .594 vs .564). With the same gpt-oss backbone, BinEval averages .539 and G-Eval .541.
- **QAGS** (App. D.2, Table 8): BinEval (Claude) has the best average r / ρ / τ:
  .604 / .620 / .534, against G-Eval (GPT-4) at .599 / .611 / .525. On gpt-oss, BinEval reaches
  .543 / .563 / .492 and G-Eval only .140 / .132 / .131. The paper attributes the G-Eval number to
  its "binary prompt [which] produces too little score granularity" (§5.3).
- **Distribution shape** (Figs. 1, 2, 7–10): BinEval's score distributions sit closest to the human
  ones and avoid the ceiling compression of UniEval and G-Eval. The figures support this
  qualitatively, but no distributional distance (for example Wasserstein or KL) is reported.

### 4.2 Why decomposition works (§5.6)

The paper names three mechanisms and backs them with item statistics:

- **Complexity reduction.** The 7 consistency questions have yes-rates from 0.75 to 0.95. The
  yes-rate spreads are 0.48 for fluency, 0.46 for relevance and 0.86 for coherence.
- **Variance reduction by aggregation.** The mean inter-question φ is 0.20 for relevance, 0.28 for
  coherence, 0.39 for fluency and 0.58 for consistency. For relevance and coherence, 80% and 64% of
  question pairs have |φ| < 0.3. The overall mean off-diagonal φ is 0.38 (Fig. 3).
- **Coverage of failure modes.** Fluency Q2 (spelling) and Q3 (punctuation) have φ = 0.02 and
  yes-rates of 0.71 vs 0.33, so they catch disjoint failures.
- **Takeaway.** Consistency gains the most over UniEval (+0.195 ρ) despite the highest φ.
  The paper reads this as "complexity reduction alone … can be the dominant driver". Practitioners
  are told to "inspect generated questions for these properties (yes-rate spread, inter-question
  correlation, pairwise coverage)".

### 4.3 Prompt update (Part II)

SummEval evaluator prompt, test ρ (Table 2, §5.4.1). The caption states: "The best iteration is
selected by early stopping on test performance."

| Dim | Self base → best | Δ | Cross-model base → best | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coherence | .521 → .610 | +.089 | .524 → .594 | +.070 |
| Consistency | .477 → .568 | +.091 | .501 → .637 | +.136 |
| Fluency | .255 → .375 | +.119 | .246 → .318 | +.072 |
| Relevance | .505 → .505 | .000 | .532 → .532 | .000 |
| Average | .440 → .515 | +.075 | .451 → .520 | +.070 |

- **Gains come early.** "most gains appear within the first one or two iterations; later
  iterations are more likely to degrade the prompt as lessons accumulate into competing
  instructions" (§5.4.1).
- **Regenerating questions matters.** "the largest gains occur in iterations that alter not only
  the evaluator prompt but also the induced question decomposition" (§5.4.1).
- **Relevance can get worse.** It reads .000 in Table 2 only because test-set early stopping
  discarded the bad iteration. App. A.2.3 shows the applied lessons dropped ρ from .505 to .357.
  The lessons "Require the evaluator to check for every key actor, every motivation, and every
  background event".

IFBench generator prompt, strict test accuracy (Tables 3, 4 and 6; §5.4.2; App. A.2.4):

- **Self-update:** 34.6 → 36.8 → 34.6 → **38.0** → 26.1 over iterations 0–4.
- **Cross-model:** 35.9 → 33.8, stopped after the second iteration.
- **No optimization:** 35.5.
- **By category:** format 52 → 69 and sentence 25 → 42. Count, ratio and repeat do not change, and
  words moves only 16 → 20. Count falls to 31 at iteration 4 (Table 6).
- **Prompt growth:** 22 → 1,841 → 3,425 → 4,890 → 6,248 characters, at 10 lessons per iteration.
  The paper calls this a "carrying capacity for prompt-based optimization" (App. A.2.4).
- **Main takeaway** (§5.4.2): prompt update "is effective when the model already has the relevant
  capability but needs better guidance to express it", and much less so "when failures reflect an
  underlying capability limitation".

## 5. Limitations the authors state (§6)

- **Failure modes:** "Decomposition works best for concrete criteria such as factual consistency
  … It is less reliable for subjective qualities, where human judgments are more holistic."
- **Computational cost:** "BinEval trades efficiency for diagnostic value." The paper gives no
  numbers for calls, tokens or latency.
- **Limitations:** "The method still depends on question quality: if important criteria are
  missing, the final score will miss them. It also assumes that the fraction of satisfied questions
  maps approximately linearly to overall quality, which need not always hold."
- **Impact statement:** evaluators "can inherit the biases and blind spots of the underlying
  language models".

## 6. Threats to validity (our reading)

### 6.1 Model selection on the test set

Table 2 and App. B ("stop when the test-set Spearman ρ decreases") pick the reported iteration
using the same data the result is reported on. The +0.075 and +0.070 averages are therefore
optimistic, and the relevance row hides a −0.148 drop. coding.sh's `src/judge/calibrate.ts` would
label this number `in-sample`.

### 6.2 Backbone confound in the headline comparison

The G-Eval (GPT-4) and UniEval (T5) rows match the numbers in the G-Eval paper (2303.16634,
Tables 1–3), so they come from 2023 runs. BinEval's best numbers use Claude Sonnet 4. The only
same-backbone comparison is on gpt-oss-120b:

| Benchmark | BinEval vs G-Eval (gpt-oss), avg ρ | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| SummEval | .447 vs .436 | +.011 |
| Topical-Chat | .539 vs .541 | −.002 |
| QAGS | .563 vs .132 | +.431 |

There is no G-Eval (Claude) run. The claim that is supported holds for one backbone: decomposition
beats a single holistic or single Boolean prompt on factual consistency, and makes little
difference on the other benchmarks.

### 6.3 Weak re-implemented baselines

On QAGS, G-Eval (gpt-oss) uses a binary prompt, and its XSum correlations are identical across
metrics (r = ρ = τ = 0.236). That indicates near-constant two-valued output. The original G-Eval
uses probability-weighted scores, and this version does not. UniEval (gpt-oss) is a single yes/no
question. Both baselines are weaker than the methods they stand for.

### 6.4 No uncertainty estimates

The paper reports no confidence intervals and no significance tests, and every number is the mean
of two runs at temperature 0. Rough intervals we computed (Fisher z for single pooled correlations,
Wilson for proportions):

- QAGS-XSum ρ 0.539, n = 239: about [0.44, 0.62].
- SummEval Table 2 test ρ 0.515, n = 1,440: about [0.48, 0.55], against the baseline's
  [0.40, 0.48]. Those are marginal intervals; a paired test on the same items would be tighter,
  but none is given.
- IFBench 38.0%, about 90 of 238: Wilson [31.9%, 44.1%], which contains the 34.6% baseline.
- The three "no change" IFBench baselines are 34.6, 35.5 and 35.9. That spread of 1.3 pp shows
  run-to-run noise of the same order as part of the claimed gain.

### 6.5 Reporting inconsistencies

- **G-Eval fluency.** The SummEval G-Eval (GPT-4) fluency cell (.506 / .455) differs from the
  G-Eval paper (.455 / .378).
- **Topical-Chat baseline labels.** The baseline numbers are copied from G-Eval's Table 2, whose
  column header reads r / ρ while its caption reads ρ / τ. BinEval prints them as ρ / τ. Kendall
  is therefore not comparable across rows, and G-Eval's Spearman average may be .588 rather than
  .575. BinEval's .632 still leads.
- **Case-study arithmetic.** Two of the four case studies don't follow §3.2's rescaling:
  - Fig. 4 maps 3/7 to 1.57 on a 1–5 scale; the formula gives 2.71.
  - The coherence case maps 7/8 to 4.56; the formula gives 4.50.

  The fluency case (2/8 → 1.50) only works on a 1–3 scale.
- **Question counts.** The fluency and relevance cases use 8 and 6 questions, while App. E lists 7
  and 5.
- **Error count.** Fig. 4's text says the summary has "three distinct factual errors", while its
  table says "Catches 4 of 7 errors", conflating questions with errors.
- **IFBench totals.** 56 + 238 = 294, not 290.

None of these change the qualitative story. Together they mean individual numbers should not be
cited without checking.

### 6.6 Other threats

- **Question-level validity is never measured.** Only aggregate scores are correlated with humans.
  We don't know how often a single "no" is correct.
- **Untested aggregation.** No weighting, gating or learned aggregation is compared with the
  unweighted mean.
- **Model as ground truth.** Cross-model update treats a model as the reference, so it can import
  that model's biases. The consistency gain (+0.136) is measured against humans, which is good,
  but the loop optimizes toward the model.
- **Narrow domain.** The domains are short-form NLG (news summaries, chit-chat) plus IFBench. None
  involves agents, tools, multi-turn trajectories or code. Agents are named only as future work
  (§7).
- **Not reproducible.** No code, and no meta-prompt.

### 6.7 Prior art the paper omits, and counter-evidence

- **Prior art not cited.** §2 says atomic-decomposition methods "typically decompose generated
  content rather than evaluation criteria themselves". Three earlier works already decompose
  *criteria* into yes/no questions, and the bibliography cites none of them (it has no
  "checklist" entries):
  - CheckEval (Lee et al., arXiv 2403.18771, EMNLP 2025), on the same SummEval and Topical-Chat
    benchmarks;
  - TICK (Cook et al., arXiv 2410.03608);
  - "Checklists Are Better Than Reward Models" (Viswanathan et al., arXiv 2507.18624).

  The novelty is narrower than §1 claims. What is new is the two-step requirements → questions
  meta-prompt and the disagreement-driven prompt update.
- **Direct replication with the opposite result.** "Ask, Then Grade: When Graded Scales Beat
  Binary Decomposition for LLM-as-Judge Evaluation" (Nandwani and Gakhar, NeurIPS 2026 workshop
  "Can We Trust the Judge?"; https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/161241; abstract only, the OpenReview
  PDF was not reachable) uses the same three benchmarks and six judge models.
  - Five-point ratings get "the highest mean Spearman correlation across benchmarks for every
    judge", and beat a six-question BinEval-style decomposition in "31 of 36 planned contrasts".
  - Its conclusion: binary decomposition "remains useful for atomic verification and
    question-level diagnosis" but is not "a generally superior default" for holistic constructs.

  This agrees with our reading of §6.2: the gain is real for atomic, checkable criteria and not
  general.
- **Citing work so far.** Semantic Scholar, checked 2026-11-06, lists 7 citing papers. The only
  one on evaluation methodology is Luo and Gao, "Designing Reliable LLM-as-a-Judge Measurement
  Systems for Multi-Turn Business Agents" (arXiv 2609.33955). It uses modular judges with human
  audits that route disagreements to label fixes, guideline fixes or judge fixes.

## 7. Mapping to coding.sh

Current state, from reading the code:

- **`src/judge/judge.ts` and `src/judge/prompt.ts`:**
  - One holistic judge call per case returns `{score 0..1, pass, confidence, reason, citations}`.
  - The version id hashes provider, model, prompt, rubric, criterion and params.
  - Each citation is checked against what the judge was shown (`verifyCitations`).
- **`src/judge/calibrate.ts`:**
  - Reports binary pass/fail κ with a bootstrap CI, the confusion matrix with "fail" as the
    positive class, and fail precision and recall.
  - Uses a held-out split and the `tunedOn` split hygiene; the default κ threshold is 0.6 and
    `minN` is 20.
- **`src/eval/judge.ts`:** a second, older `calibrate` with κ ≥ 0.7 and `minN` 10. It ignores
  splits.
- **`src/stats`:** Wilson intervals, a paired cluster bootstrap and exact McNemar.
- **`src/eval/validate.ts`:**
  - Runs the no-op, reference k/k, near-miss and leakage checks.
  - Near-misses come from `nearMissMutations` in `src/eval/patch.ts`, which drops one hunk of the
    reference patch at a time.

### 7.1 Adopt

**A1. Checklist judges: binary items, frozen, versioned.** Touches `src/judge`,
`src/graders/index.ts`, `src/datasets/types.ts`, and the ARCHITECTURE "Graders" and "Judge
calibration" sections.

1. Add `LlmChecklistSpec` (`kind: "llm-checklist"`) next to `LlmGraderSpec`, with
   `items: ChecklistItem[]`:

   ```ts
   interface ChecklistItem {
     id: string;                 // stable, e.g. "verified-before-done"
     dimension?: string;         // partition, as Q_d in §3.1
     question: string;           // "yes" = satisfied
     violationExample?: string;  // §3.1 Step 2
     critical?: boolean;         // a "no" fails the case regardless of the mean
     weight?: number;            // default 1
     check?: TracePredicateRef;  // deterministic check; skips the LLM (A2)
   }
   ```

2. Per item, `JudgeVerdict` gains
   `items: { id, answer: boolean | null, explanation, citations, source: "predicate" | "llm" }[]`.
3. `score` stays a weighted mean in [0, 1] (§3.2). `pass` requires every `critical` item to be
   "yes" and `score ≥ passThreshold`.
4. `judgeVersionFor` hashes the ordered item list. If the questions were generated by an LLM, the
   meta-prompt hash and generator model join the hash too. Questions are generated once at authoring
   time and stored. They are never regenerated at eval time: the paper regenerates them per
   iteration, which makes the judge nondeterministic across runs.
5. Default to one call per item, to match "answers them independently" (§3). Add a
   `mode: "batched"` option that answers all items in one structured call for cost, and record the
   mode in the version hash.

**A2. "Check, then ask": route items to deterministic predicates first.** Touches a new
`src/judge/predicates.ts`, plus `src/core/sessions.ts` for event access. Many coding-agent
criteria are facts about the trace, not opinions:

- ran the tests after the last edit;
- edited a test file;
- ran a destructive command without asking;
- claimed completion with no passing test afterwards;
- reached the tool through a help lookup first.

Give these as named predicates over `AgentEvent[]` and the tool-eval tap records, and let a
checklist item reference one through `check`. Only items without a predicate go to the LLM. This
goes further than the paper's thesis: the most trustworthy answer to a binary question is a
program. It also matches DOGFOOD §4 guard 3, "Programmatic over judged". The DOGFOOD §3.2
harvest flags (`premature-completion`, `repeated-tool-failure`, `no-verification`) become the
first predicates.

**A3. Per-item diagnostics (§5.6).** Touches a new `src/judge/diagnostics.ts`; add `phi()` to
`src/stats/agreement.ts`. Over a judged set, report:

- yes-rate per item, flagging items with yes-rate < 0.05 or > 0.95 as near-constant, since they
  carry little information;
- φ between items, flagging pairs with φ > 0.8 as redundant;
- yes-rate spread per dimension;
- the ceiling share: the fraction of cases at score 1.0, which surfaces the ceiling effect the
  paper criticizes;
- the gap between judge and human fail rates (already computed as `humanFailRate` and
  `judgeFailRate` in `SplitMetrics`; surface it).

Show these in `coding.sh judge calibrate` output and in the viewer.

**A4. Question-level disagreement for judge migration (§3.3).** Touches `src/judge` and the CLI.
Add `coding.sh judge diff <judgeA> <judgeB> --dataset D`, which reports per item:

- the disagreement rate;
- κ between the two judges;
- the five cases with the most disagreement, with both explanations.

This is the useful, non-automated core of cross-model update. It shows *which criterion* a cheaper
or local judge (Ollama or LM Studio through `openAICompatibleProvider`) gets wrong compared with a
calibrated one. A migrated judge is a new version. It inherits no calibration and must pass
`calibrate()` on held-out human labels again.

**A5. Failed items as a failure taxonomy and as actionable feedback.** Touches
`src/experiments/compare.ts`, `src/viewer`, and DOGFOOD §5 (`brief.mjs`).

- **Compare.** `compareExperiments` already gives per-grader deltas. Extend them to per-item
  pass-rate deltas, each with `pairedBootstrap` over cases, so a harness A/B says *which criterion*
  moved.
- **Viewer.** Add a "top failed items" view across sessions.
- **Dogfood brief.** The brief should list the failed item ids and explanations of failing trials,
  not just the metric name. This is the paper's "actionable feedback" claim (§6.1), applied where
  we already have a consumer.

**A6. Requirement-targeted near-misses in verifier validation.** Touches `src/eval/validate.ts`,
`src/eval/patch.ts`, `ValidationReport` in `src/core/types.ts`, and ARCHITECTURE "Evals" step 3.
The paper's §3.1 pipeline (task prompt → requirements → one binary question per requirement, each
with a violation example) maps directly onto `eval new`:

1. Extract the requirements from the instruction, plus any follow-up prompts in
   `task.meta.followUps`, and store them in `task.meta.requirements`. Freeze them and let a human
   edit them in review.
2. For each requirement, produce a near-miss that violates *only that requirement*. Use a hunk
   drop from `nearMissMutations` when one hunk maps to the requirement. Otherwise use an
   LLM-written violation patch, stored as a blob.
3. Add `requirementCoverage: { caught: number; of: number; uncaught: string[] }` to
   `ValidationReport`. A verifier that passes the violation for requirement r does not test r.

Today's hunk-drop mutations test "is the verifier sensitive to the patch?". This change tests "is
the verifier sensitive to *each thing the user asked for*?", which is the question that matters.

### 7.2 Build differently, and why

| Paper | coding.sh | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Unweighted mean of yes answers (§3.2); "assumes … approximately linearly" (§6) | Weighted mean **plus `critical` gates** | In agent evals one "no" can be disqualifying (deleted tests, leaked secret, destructive command). The authors name linearity as a limitation. |
| Evaluator quality = Spearman/Kendall/Pearson vs Likert (§4.1) | Keep binary pass/fail **Cohen's κ on held-out labels** as the gate. Add Kendall τ-b with bootstrap CI as a diagnostic for continuous scores. | Our consumers make pass/fail and keep/reject decisions, and correlation hides threshold errors. Our calibrate already reports fail precision and recall, which correlation cannot. |
| Best iteration picked on test (Table 2, App. B) | Iterate on `train`/`dev`, stop on `dev`, report on `test` once. Any split used for early stopping is added to `tunedOn`. | Otherwise the number is in-sample. `calibrate.ts` already enforces this, so the optimizer must feed it honest `tunedOn`. |
| Stronger model as reference (§3.3) | Humans are the reference. A model reference is allowed only to *diagnose* a migration (A4), never to certify one. | A model reference imports its biases. The paper's own relevance failure shows optimizing toward a stricter judge diverges from humans. |
| Temperature 0, mean of 2 runs (§4) | Measure judge **test-retest self-agreement** (κ between two runs on a 10–20% subset), and cache verdicts by input hash (already done). | Anthropic providers reject sampling params (see the comment on `LlmGraderSpec.temperature` in `src/graders/index.ts`), so temperature 0 is not universally available. Self-agreement caps the human κ a judge can reach. |
| Automatic lesson → prompt rewriting (§3.3–3.4) | Ship the diagnosis (A4, A5) first. Add an optimizer later with hard caps: ≤ 2 iterations by default, a prompt-size budget, and a paired non-inferiority keep rule as in DOGFOOD §5. | The paper's own evidence: gains in iterations 1–2, then degradation; prompt bloat 22 → 6,248 chars; IFBench collapse to 26.1%; relevance −0.148. |
| Questions regenerated during optimization | Frozen in the judge version; regenerating makes a new version | Reproducibility, and calibration that stays attached to the right judge. |
| Questions generated from the task prompt alone (§3.1) | Draft items from the instruction **and** from captured failing vs passing trajectories of the same task; a human reviews them | Viswanathan et al. (2507.18624, Table 6) found checklists written from failure modes in candidate responses beat checklists extracted from the instruction alone: IFEval 77.3 vs 74.3. coding.sh already holds those trajectories. |
| Binary decomposition as the method | Judge format is a versioned choice per criterion: `llm` (graded) or `llm-checklist`, picked by held-out κ | "Ask, Then Grade" (§6.7) finds graded scales win for holistic constructs. Our calibration pipeline can settle it per criterion. |
| One explanation per question | Explanation **plus verified citation** to a trace event id | We already verify citations (`verifyCitations`). An item answered "no" without a verified citation should be flagged, which addresses position and verbosity bias at the item level. |

### 7.3 Where it contradicts or pressures our current specs

1. **Two calibration implementations disagree.** `src/eval/judge.ts` `calibrate()` uses κ ≥ 0.7,
   `minN` 10, and no split awareness. `src/judge/calibrate.ts` uses κ ≥ 0.6, `minN` 20, and a held-out
   split. ARCHITECTURE "Judge calibration" states neither value. The paper's test-set model
   selection is exactly what the split-blind version allows. Make `src/eval/judge.ts` delegate to
   `src/judge/calibrate.ts`, and state one threshold in ARCHITECTURE.
2. **The DOGFOOD §3.4 council uses a holistic 1–10 rubric.** That is the "opaque score" the paper
   argues against. The paper also shows holistic criteria such as relevance decompose badly
   (App. A.2.3), and site aesthetics are close to relevance. Keep the relative holistic score but
   add a binary checklist:
   - hero readable at 390×844;
   - a primary call to action above the fold;
   - no text over a busy background;
   - and so on.

   Items Lighthouse or the DOM can answer become predicates. Report the checklist
   relative score next to the holistic one, and keep the holistic weight at 4/100 or lower.
3. **The ARCHITECTURE "Evals" verifier check 3 ("generated near-misses fail")** is satisfied today by
   hunk drops, which say nothing about requirement coverage. A6 makes it requirement-based.
4. **The paper's prompt-update result supports TOOL-EVALS §8.3 and DOGFOOD §5,** but it adds one
   missing metric. Neither tracks the size of the instruction surface the loop edits:
   CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md, skills, the brief. The IFBench bloat curve (App. A.2.4, Table 6) is the
   failure mode to guard against. Add `bundleInstructionBytes` and `bundleInstructionTokens` to
   `HarnessBundle` (computed in `src/runner/bundle.ts`), and show them in compare. In the DOGFOOD
   keep rule, a candidate that grows the instruction surface by more than 20% needs a
   success-difference CI that excludes 0, not just non-inferiority.
5. **WORKFLOWS §5 "Synthesis / research → Rubric"** should name the checklist judge, with items
   partitioned by dimension, and report per-item recall in seeded mode.

### 7.4 What not to take

- Affine rescaling to Likert ranges (§3.2). We have no Likert consumers.
- The equal-weight assumption.
- Correlation as the calibration gate.
- Test-set early stopping.
- The idea that a meta-prompt can generate good questions unattended. The paper's own failure
  (App. A.2.3) is LLM-generated questions that are too strict. Generated checklists must go
  through human review, as session-derived tasks do (DOGFOOD §3.2 step 4).
